Nutrition & Weight Management

Wrestlers are strength athletes who compete in weight classes. Fueling well is how you keep strength on the mat and finish the season healthy.

Our program does not cut weight the old-fashioned way. No plastics, no saunas, no skipping meals, no spitting or dehydrating to make a class. Those methods are banned by the NFHS and UHSAA, they are dangerous, and they make you wrestle worse. If a weight class is not reachable by eating and training well, it is the wrong weight class.

Training Is the Stimulus. Food Is the Recovery.

Practice does not make you better on its own — it is the stimulus. What you eat, what you drink and how you sleep afterward is what turns that stimulus into a better wrestler. Stimulus without recovery is just overtraining: more injuries, more missed practices, worse performance in February.

Eat and drink well through a season and you get four things:

  • Full recovery from every practice, so you get the whole benefit of each session
  • Energy that lasts through the school day and into the third period
  • Far fewer practices missed to illness
  • Proper hydration, which means better performance and lower injury risk

The Macros, by the Numbers

Wrestling sits between endurance and power on the fueling scale, and the targets below are per kilogram of bodyweight — divide your weight in pounds by 2.2 to get kilograms, or use the calculator underneath.

Eating fewer calories raises the requirement, not lowers it. Chicken, beef, fish, eggs, milk, yogurt, cheese, cottage cheese, nuts, beans, peanut butter
Fats Energy stores, hormone regulation, organ protection, and the carrier for some vitamins. 20–35% of total calories, higher in heavy training blocks. Keep saturated fat under 10%. Oils, nuts, avocado, peanut butter
Fruits & vegetables Vitamins and minerals, fiber and water for digestion and steady blood sugar, for very few calories. 2–4 servings of fruit and 3–5 servings of vegetables daily — more vegetables than fruit, at least one leafy green. Apples, citrus, berries, grapes, bananas; broccoli, spinach, kale, peppers, yams, carrots, green beans, peas
Water Affects performance more than any other nutrient. Your body is about 70% water; muscle is about 75%. All day, every day — not just at practice. Pale-yellow urine is the target you can actually check. Water first. Sports drinks cut with water (the 2:1 rule). Chocolate milk occasionally, and it is excellent right after practice.

The Rules That Do the Most Work

  • Eat breakfast every day. Skipping it is the most common mistake we see.
  • Drink water all day. The handout's rule of thumb is up to a gallon on hard training days; what matters is steady sipping from a bottle you carry, with pale-yellow urine as your check. Never restrict fluids to make weight.
  • Five fruits and vegetables a day.
  • A lean protein source at every meal.
  • Four to six smaller meals a day rather than two big ones.
  • Eat within an hour of finishing a workout. This is the single highest-value habit on the list.
  • Go easy on saturated fat, simple sugars and caffeine.

A Day of Fueling

WhenWhat it doesExamples
Breakfast Sets up your whole day. Oatmeal with peanut butter and banana; eggs and toast; Greek yogurt with granola and berries.
Lunch Keeps energy up through afternoon classes and into practice. Turkey or chicken sandwich, fruit, milk, pretzels; rice bowl with chicken and veggies.
Pre-practice (30–60 min before) Quick carbs so you can go hard for the whole practice. Banana, granola bar, applesauce pouch, crackers, a handful of dried fruit.
Right after practice (within the hour) The recovery window — refills glycogen and starts muscle repair. Chocolate milk, protein shake with fruit, yogurt and granola, PB&J.
Dinner The real recovery meal. Protein + carbs + vegetables. Chicken, rice and broccoli; spaghetti with meat sauce and salad; tacos with beans and rice.

Easy Swaps

Same meal, better fuel. None of these cost anything extra:

  • Baked, grilled or broiled meat instead of fried
  • Meat skinless and unbreaded
  • Fresh fruit and vegetables instead of canned or processed
  • Skin on where you can — potatoes especially
  • Vegetables raw or steamed
  • Oil-based dressings instead of cream-based
  • Red sauces instead of white sauces
  • White cheeses instead of yellow or orange
  • Jelly, peanut butter or honey on toast and bagels instead of butter or cream cheese
  • Skip the added butter, salt and sugar
  • Water instead of soda — every time
  • Pair incomplete proteins: beans and rice, corn and beans, peanut butter and bread

Competition Day

  • Weigh-in morning: eat something small and familiar after you step off the scale — do not experiment with new food on match day.
  • Right after weigh-ins: rehydrate steadily (water plus a sports drink) and eat easy carbs with a little protein: bagel with peanut butter, banana, granola bar, rice, a sandwich.
  • Between matches: keep sipping fluids and grazing on light snacks. Fruit, applesauce, pretzels, sports drink. Avoid greasy or heavy food that sits in your stomach.
  • Tournament bag checklist: two water bottles, a sports drink, fruit, a sandwich, granola bars, nut butter packets, pretzels. Concessions run out and rarely have what you need.
  • After the last match: real meal within an hour. You may wrestle again tomorrow.

Making Weight the Right Way

Utah high school wrestling uses the NFHS weight management program. Every wrestler is assessed at the start of the season for body composition and hydration, which sets the lowest weight class you are allowed to certify at, and limits how fast you can descend to it (roughly a percentage and a half of body weight per week). The program exists to protect you — work inside it, not around it. Your coaches have your current certification and descent plan; ask them where you stand.

  • Pick a class you can hit hydrated, three days out, with a normal breakfast in you.
  • Lose weight over weeks with training and consistent eating, never overnight.
  • Never restrict fluids. Ever.
  • Cutting carbs to make weight is the fastest way to gas out in the third period.
  • Weigh yourself before and after practice to learn how much fluid you lose — then replace it.
  • Tell a coach if making your class feels like it is taking over your life.

Speak up early. Skipping meals, throwing up, laxatives, fasting or obsessive weighing are not toughness — they are warning signs, and they end seasons and careers. If you or a teammate is struggling with food or weight, talk to a coach, the athletic trainer, the school counselor or your family. That conversation is always confidential and you will never lose your spot for having it.

Sleep and Recovery

Eight to nine hours a night is part of your training plan. Sleep is when your body rebuilds what practice broke down. Combine it with an honest warm-up, a real cool-down and reporting soreness or skin issues to the trainer early, and you will still be healthy in February when it matters most.
